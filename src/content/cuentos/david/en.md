The Bible tells of a boy named David who lived long, long ago in the hills around Bethlehem. He was the youngest of eight brothers, and he spent his days outdoors, looking after his father Jesse’s sheep. His hair was tousled by the wind, his hands were brown from the sun, and a leather sling hung from his belt.

David loved his work. In the morning he led the flock to the meadows where the grass was softest, and in the afternoon he guided them down to the stream for a drink of cool water. He knew every single sheep: the one who always lagged behind, the one who bleated the loudest, and the little lamb who never left its mother’s side.

When the sun went down, David sat on a rock and played his harp. His songs were about the starry sky, the wind in the olive trees, and God, who took care of him just as he took care of his sheep. The flock huddled around him to listen.

Some nights called for courage. If a wild animal prowled near the flock, David didn’t run away. He shouted, waved his staff, and chased it far from the lambs. Then he went back to the fire and whispered, “God helps me protect the ones I care for.”

Meanwhile, his three oldest brothers were far from home with King Saul’s army. The soldiers of Israel had set up camp on one hill, and across a valley, on another hill, the Philistine army was waiting. One day Jesse called his youngest son. “David, take this roasted grain and these loaves to your brothers. See how they’re doing, and come back and tell me.”

David left the sheep with another shepherd he trusted, swung the sack over his shoulder, and walked to the camp. It was bustling with tents, shields flashing in the sun, and soldiers coming and going. He found his brothers and gave them the food. But suddenly the whole camp went quiet.

From the Philistine side came an enormous man. His name was Goliath, and he was so tall he looked like a tower. He wore a bronze helmet and armor that clinked with every step, and he carried a spear as long as a tree trunk. He planted his feet in the middle of the valley and roared like thunder: “Won’t anyone face me? Send just one of you down here!”

The soldiers of Israel looked at the ground. Some took a step back. No one answered. It had been like this for forty days.

“Why doesn’t anyone answer him?” asked David. “God is with us!” His oldest brother, Eliab, frowned. “What are you doing here? Go back to your sheep.” But David didn’t leave, and his words passed from soldier to soldier until they reached the king’s ears.

King Saul sent for him. When he saw how young David was, he sighed. “You can’t go up against that giant. You’re hardly more than a boy, and he has been a soldier all his life.” David answered calmly, “Your Majesty, I look after my father’s sheep. Whenever a lion or a bear came near the flock, God gave me the strength to defend it. He will help me now too.”

Saul slowly nodded. “Go, and may God be with you.” Then he offered David his own armor. They put the bronze helmet on his head, the metal coat on his shoulders, and the sword at his side. David took one step, then another, and nearly toppled over. Everything was heavy and tight, and it went clink, clank with every move. “I can’t walk in this,” he said with a smile. “I’m not used to it.” And he took it all off.

He picked up his staff and his sling and went down to the stream that ran through the valley. He crouched by the cold water and searched patiently. He chose five stones, smooth and round, polished by the water. One, two, three, four, five. He tucked them into his shepherd’s bag.

Goliath saw the boy coming and let out a laugh that echoed around the hills. “Is this your champion? He’s just a little lad with a stick!” David kept walking. In a clear voice he replied, “You trust in your spear and your armor. I trust in God, and that is stronger than any sword.”

The giant came forward with steps that made the ground shake. David ran toward him, reached into his bag, took out a stone, and placed it in his sling. He swung it around once, twice, three times, whoosh, whoosh, whoosh, and let it fly.

The stone sailed straight and swift and struck Goliath on the forehead. The giant wobbled, his eyes went wide, and he crashed face-first to the ground with an enormous thud, like an old tree toppling over. There he lay in the dust, with no strength left to get up.

The Philistines stared at one another in amazement. If their strongest warrior had fallen to a boy with a sling, what could they do? They turned around and hurried away, down the hillside, until they disappeared over the horizon. For a moment the valley was silent, and then it burst into cheers.

The soldiers of Israel crowded around David and lifted him up in their arms. His brothers ran to hug him, and Eliab ruffled his hair. “I’m sorry, little brother. You were braver than all of us.”

That night, beside a campfire, David took out his harp and sang a new song. It wasn’t about spears or giants. It was about a God who stays close to little ones when they are afraid. In his bag there were still four smooth stones from the stream, and David smiled when he felt them there.

Many years later, that young shepherd would become king of Israel. But he never forgot the stream in the valley, or what he learned that day: you don’t have to be the biggest to be brave.
