According to the Bible, long ago, in the city of Jericho, there lived a man named Zacchaeus. Jericho was a green and fragrant city, full of towering palm trees, fig orchards and market stalls piled with dates as sweet as honey. But Zacchaeus hardly ever stopped to enjoy any of it.

Zacchaeus was the chief tax collector. His job was to collect the money people had to pay to the emperor in Rome. And Zacchaeus took more than he should. If someone owed three coins, he asked for five, and he kept the extra two in his own purse. That was how he had a big house with a courtyard and a fountain, and clothes in expensive colors.

But nobody in Jericho liked Zacchaeus. When he walked down the street, the stallholders looked away, the neighbors shut their windows and the children stopped playing. Zacchaeus was very short, and sometimes he felt that people looked down on him from way up high, as if he were even smaller than he was. At night, alone in his big house, he wondered, “Who would ever want to be friends with someone like me?”

One day, the whole city woke up in a flurry. “Jesus is coming! He’s passing through Jericho!” people shouted everywhere. Everyone had heard about him: about the sick people he healed, the stories he told, and the kind way he treated everyone, especially the people nobody else was kind to.

Zacchaeus felt a flutter in his chest. He wanted very much to see Jesus, even if only for a moment. So he left his accounts on the table and went out to the main street.

But the street was packed. There were tall men, women with water jars on their heads, and children riding on their parents’ shoulders. Zacchaeus stood on tiptoe: all he could see were backs. He gave a little jump: all he could see were headscarves. He tried to squeeze between two neighbors, but they pressed their elbows together and wouldn’t let him through. “Back of the line, Zacchaeus,” one of them said with a scowl.

Zacchaeus stood still for a moment, his cheeks burning. Then he looked past the crowd and saw, at the end of the street, an enormous sycamore fig tree. It had a thick trunk and low branches, with broad leaves like open hands. Jesus would have to walk right underneath it.

Without a second thought, Zacchaeus hitched up his robe and started to run. He ran on his short legs, dodging goats and baskets, while some people laughed to see him go by. Such a rich, serious man, running like a little boy! He reached the tree out of breath, grabbed the lowest branch and climbed, and climbed, until he was hidden among the leaves, his heart leaping.

From up there he could see everything. He saw the dusty road, the tops of people’s heads and, at last, Jesus, walking slowly with his friends all around him. Zacchaeus held his breath. “I just want to look at him,” he thought. “Nobody will notice I’m here.”

But just as he reached the sycamore, Jesus stopped. The whole crowd stopped with him. Jesus lifted his head, looked up through the broad leaves and found Zacchaeus’s eyes. And then he called him by his name.

“Zacchaeus, hurry and come down,” he said with a smile. “Today I want to stay at your house.”

Zacchaeus nearly fell out of the tree. Jesus knew his name? Jesus wanted to go to his house, the house everyone else stayed away from? He climbed down so fast that he scraped his knees, but he didn’t care. He was so happy that the joy could hardly fit inside him.

The crowd, though, began to mutter. The words passed from mouth to mouth like an angry wind. “Did you see? He’s going to Zacchaeus’s house.” “After everything that man has taken from us!” “How can he sit at the table of someone like that?” Zacchaeus heard every word, and every word stung a little, because he knew it was true.

That afternoon, in the courtyard of the big house, beside the fountain, Zacchaeus served bread, olives, figs and dates. Jesus ate and talked with him the way you talk with a friend. And Zacchaeus, who had always counted coins, began to count something else: the people he had hurt. The woman who sold figs. The fisherman from the river. The widow who lived by the city wall.

Suddenly, Zacchaeus stood up. He seemed taller than ever. “Lord,” he said, in a voice that trembled but stood firm, “I am going to give half of everything I own to the poor. And to everyone I charged too much, I will pay back four times what I took.”

Jesus looked at him with enormous joy. He said that salvation had come to that house that very day, and that he had come for exactly this: to look for people who had lost their way and bring them back home.

The next morning, Jericho saw something it had never seen before: Zacchaeus walking through the streets with a bag full of coins, knocking on doors. The woman who sold figs opened her mouth and couldn’t think of a thing to say. The fisherman counted his coins three times. The widow by the wall burst into happy tears and gave him a hug that squeezed all the breath out of him.

Zacchaeus stayed short for the rest of his life. But from that day on, when he walked down the street, the stallholders waved, the neighbors opened their windows and the children called him over to play. And sometimes, as he passed the sycamore, Zacchaeus would pat its trunk and smile, remembering the day someone looked up at him and called him by his name.
