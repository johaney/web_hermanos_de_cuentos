On a narrow street in an old town lived a shoemaker named Martin and his wife, Elvira. Martin was a fine craftsman and an honest man: he never charged too much, never used poor leather and always mended children’s sandals for free. But that year things were going badly, and every week they had a little less money.

One autumn evening, Martin opened the cupboard in his workshop and saw that he had only enough leather left for one pair of shoes. “This is the last of it,” he told Elvira. Very carefully, he cut out the pieces and laid them on his workbench, ready to sew in the morning. Then he blew out the lamp and went to bed, feeling a little worried.

The next day, when he came down to the workshop, he rubbed his eyes. On the bench stood a finished pair of shoes. The stitches were so tiny and so straight that they looked as if they had been sewn with a thread of light. “Elvira, come and see this!” he called. His wife came down in her nightgown and stared with her mouth open. Neither of them knew who had made them.

That very morning, a lady in a feathered hat came into the shop. She saw the shoes in the window, tried them on, and they fitted perfectly. She was so pleased that she paid more than they cost. With that money, Martin was able to buy leather for two pairs.

That night he cut out the pieces, left them on the bench and went to bed. And the next morning, there they were: two pairs of finished shoes, shining and beautifully stitched. He sold them straight away and bought leather for four pairs. The next night the same thing happened, and the night after that, and the night after that.

Soon people all over town were talking about Martin’s shoe shop. Bakers, teachers, musicians and even a ship’s captain came to order boots. Once again there was wood for the fire, bread on the table and roasted chestnuts on Sundays. But Martin and Elvira never forgot that someone was helping them.

One December night, shortly before Christmas, Elvira said, “Martin, what if we stay up to see who’s been helping us?” Martin thought it was a good idea. They lit a small candle, set it in a corner and hid behind the curtain by the stairs.

When the church clock struck twelve, the workshop window slowly swung open. In came two little elves, no taller than a coffee cup. They had round cheeks, pointed ears and bare feet. Their clothes were so worn that their elbows and knees poked through the holes.

The elves sprang up onto the bench, picked up the needles and began to sew as fast as they could. As they worked, they hummed softly, “Stitch goes in and stitch comes out, new shoes for those who go without.” Snip, snap, snip, snap went the needles. Before the rooster crowed, the shoes were finished, and the elves slipped out through the window.

Martin and Elvira came out from behind the curtain with shining eyes. “They’ve saved us,” whispered Elvira, “and they haven’t even got shoes, or a coat for the cold.” Martin nodded. “We must thank them. And not just with words.”

For several days, they worked in secret. Elvira sewed two little linen shirts, two green waistcoats, two pairs of woolen trousers and two red caps with a tiny bell on the tip. Martin made two pairs of little shoes from soft leather, so small they could fit inside a walnut shell. On Christmas Eve, instead of leaving leather on the bench, they left the presents there, neatly folded.

At midnight, the elves came in through the window as usual. They looked for the leather but couldn’t find it. Then they saw the tiny clothes. They looked at each other in surprise, touched them gently and, when they understood the clothes were for them, dressed in the blink of an eye. They put on their caps, slipped on their little shoes and twirled around the bench, admiring each other.

“How smart we look!” said one. “How cozy we feel!” said the other. And they began to dance among the scissors and spools of thread, jingling their bells. They leaped onto a chair, from the chair to the floor and from the floor to the window. And off they went, dancing happily through the snow, and they never came back.

Martin and Elvira missed them, but they wanted for nothing. Martin had learned so much from watching those tiny stitches that his shoes were now the best in town. And because he never forgot what it felt like to open an empty cupboard, he decided to do something about it.

Every winter, on cold nights, Martin and Elvira sewed shoes for the children in the neighborhood who had none, and left them on their doorsteps before dawn without a word. They also taught the trade to an orphan boy named Nico, who grew up to be a wonderful shoemaker.

And they say that when Martin worked late by candlelight, he sometimes hummed that song very softly: “Stitch goes in and stitch comes out, new shoes for those who go without.” And somewhere out in the snow, two little elves in red caps danced when they heard it.
