Long ago, in a city of red roofs and cobbled streets, there lived an emperor who loved clothes more than anything else in the world. He had one outfit for breakfast, another for strolling, another for feeding the ducks, and a very elegant one just for taking naps. His wardrobe was so big it had its own staircase.

Whenever someone at the palace asked, “Where is the emperor?” the answer was always the same: “Trying something on.”

One day, two strangers with wide smiles and quick eyes arrived in the city. They called themselves Master Tack and Master Thimble, and they said they were the finest weavers in the world. “Our cloth is the most beautiful there is,” they explained at the palace gate. “And it has a secret: it is invisible to anyone who is foolish, or who doesn’t deserve their job.”

The emperor’s eyes lit up. “How marvelous!” he thought. “With clothes like that, I’ll know who is clever in my palace and who isn’t.” And he gave them two sacks of gold coins, skeins of silk and spools of golden thread so they could start at once.

The weavers packed everything away in their trunks and set up two looms in a room of the palace. There they spent all day moving their hands through the air, without a single thread. Click, clack, click, clack went the empty looms from morning until night.

After a week, the emperor grew curious, but also a tiny bit worried. “What if I can’t see it?” he thought. So he decided to send his old minister first, who was honest and very sensible.

The minister entered the room, straightened his glasses and looked at the looms. There was nothing there. Absolutely nothing. “Oh dear,” he thought. “Could I be foolish? Could it be that I don’t deserve my job?” The weavers came closer. “Isn’t it lovely? Just look at this sea blue and this peacock embroidery.” And the minister, who didn’t want anyone to think badly of him, said, “Lovely! Lovely!”

Next went the general, and the same thing happened to him. He didn’t see anything either, and he didn’t want to say so either. “Lovely! Lovely!” he repeated, and marched out of the room very stiffly. Soon the whole city was talking about the marvelous cloth, and everyone was eager to find out which of their neighbors was foolish.

At last, the emperor went himself, with his whole court. He looked at the looms and gulped. He couldn’t see a single thread. “Could I be foolish? Could it be that I don’t deserve to be emperor?” he thought in alarm. But since his minister and his general had already praised it, he smiled and said, “It’s magnificent!” And the whole court clapped at once: “Lovely! Lovely!”

It was decided that the emperor would wear his new clothes for the first time in the great spring parade. The night before, the weavers lit twenty candles and worked without stopping. They snipped the air with enormous scissors and sewed with needles that had no thread.

The next morning, they dressed the emperor in front of the big mirror. “Raise your arms, Your Majesty. Here are the trousers. Here is the jacket. And here is the cape, as light as a spider’s web.” The emperor took off his usual clothes and stood there in his undershirt and a pair of long polka-dot underpants. “You may keep your underclothes on,” said the weavers, “because our cloth is so fine that otherwise you’d catch a chill.” The emperor turned this way and that in front of the mirror, very pleased. Two pages bent down and lifted the invisible train from the floor, holding their hands up high, as if they were carrying something very delicate.

And so the parade began. Trumpets blared and drums rolled, and the emperor walked beneath a velvet canopy with his chin held high. People leaned out of balconies and crowded onto the sidewalks. Nobody could see the clothes, but nobody wanted to look foolish. “What a long train!” they said. “What embroidery!”

In the middle of the crowd, sitting on his father’s shoulders, was a boy named Martin. He looked at the emperor, rubbed his eyes, looked again, and said in a loud, clear voice, “But the emperor’s in his underpants!”

There was a silence. Then someone whispered, “He’s in his underpants.” And someone else said, “He’s in polka-dot underpants!” The words hopped from person to person until the whole square was saying them and laughing.

The emperor felt a cool little breeze around his knees and turned as red as a tomato. He looked down at his underpants. He looked at the crowd. He looked at the boy. And then, instead of getting angry, he let out a laugh so big that his crown wobbled. “He’s right! I’m in my underpants! And polka-dot ones, too!” The whole city laughed along with him, and it was the happiest laughter anyone had heard in years.

When he could finally stop laughing, the emperor called the boy over. “You were the only one who said what you saw,” he told him. “From today on, you will be my Counselor of Truth.” And from then on, every Monday, the emperor would ask him, “Martin, what do you see?” And Martin always told him.

And the weavers? They had slipped away at dawn with their trunks full of gold, but the trunks were so heavy that their mules plodded along terribly slowly, and the guards caught up with them at the bridge. They had to give everything back and spend a whole year weaving, for real this time, warm blankets for every family in the city.

The emperor gave away half of his clothes and spent his spare time listening to his people. And they say that every spring, the city celebrates Polka-Dot Day. Everyone wears something with polka dots, the emperor first of all, and for one whole day, everybody laughs a little at themselves.
