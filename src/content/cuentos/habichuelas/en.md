Jack and his mother lived in a little cottage at the end of the village, with a small vegetable patch and a white cow named Daisy. That year it had hardly rained at all. The garden was dry, the pantry was empty, and Daisy had stopped giving milk.

At night, to keep Jack from thinking about how hungry he was, his mother told him stories. His favorite was the one about his grandfather. “Your grandfather had a hen that laid golden eggs and a harp that sang all by itself,” she would say. “He used the gold to help the whole village, and the harp sang at every celebration. But one stormy night, a giant came down from the clouds and took them away.” Jack always asked the same question: “And didn’t anyone go to get them back?” And his mother always sighed: “The clouds are very high, my love.”

One morning his mother said sadly, “You’ll have to take Daisy to market. Don’t take less than ten coins for her.” Jack patted the cow and set off.

At a crossroads, sitting on a stone, was an old man in a hat full of feathers. “What a fine cow,” he said. “I’ll trade you for these.” He opened his hand and showed Jack five beans that shimmered with every color, like soap bubbles. “They’re magic. If you plant them, they’ll take you to where your family’s lost treasures are.”

Jack hesitated. But the old man looked at Daisy so fondly that Jack felt sure he would take good care of her. “With me she’ll have the greenest meadow in the world,” the old man promised. And Jack agreed.

When his mother saw the five beans, she clutched her head. “Five beans for our Daisy! Oh, Jack, Jack!” And she threw them out of the window in a huff. That night they ate a very thin soup without saying a word.

The next morning, Jack’s room was full of green light. He looked out of the window. In the vegetable patch stood a plant with a stalk as thick as a tower, growing up and up until it disappeared into the clouds.

“I’m going to climb it,” said Jack. “Don’t you dare!” cried his mother. Jack gulped. He was afraid of heights, but he thought of his grandfather, the hen and the harp. “I’m scared, Mother, but I’m going to climb it. I’ll be very careful.” His mother hugged him tight. “I’ll wait for you down here,” she said. “With the axe close by, just in case.”

And Jack began to climb. The leaves were as big as umbrellas, and the wind tousled his hair. “One leaf, then another, and don’t look down,” he told himself. “One leaf, then another, and don’t look down.”

At the top, the clouds were as solid as grass. A white road led to an enormous castle, with a door as tall as the village church.

Inside, everything was huge. The chairs were like houses, the spoons were like boats, and on the table sat a loaf of bread the size of a cart. In an armchair, the giant was fast asleep, wearing a nightcap, with a beard like a haystack. He snored so loudly that the windows rattled. In a cage on the table was a hen with cinnamon-colored feathers. And on a shelf, tied with a chain, was a harp with golden strings and a flower carved into the wood. The very same flower that was carved on the door of Jack’s house!

Suddenly the giant opened one eye. “Grrr! Who’s there? How many times have I said nobody disturbs my nap?” Jack leaped inside a giant slipper to hide. The giant yawned. “Hen, lay an egg.” The hen, looking very sad, laid a golden egg, and the giant tossed it onto a glittering heap in the corner. “More gold, more gold, and none of it’s any use to me,” he grumbled. “Harp, sing.” The harp sang a lullaby so soft that the giant was snoring again in no time.

Then the harp whispered, “Boy, have you come from down below? I used to sing at the village celebrations. Please, take us home.” The hen clucked quietly, as if to say yes.

Jack carefully undid the chain, slung the harp over his back, picked up the cage and tiptoed toward the door. But the hen was so happy she couldn’t hold it in: “Cluck, cluck, clu-u-uck!” The giant woke with a start. “Hey! My hen! My harp!” Jack turned around for a moment. “They’re not yours! They belong to my village!” he shouted, and he ran.

The giant came after him, but he was slow and still half asleep. He yawned with every step and kept tripping over his nightgown. Jack reached the beanstalk and climbed down as fast as he could. “Mother, the axe!” he called. “Mother, the axe!”

His mother was already waiting at the bottom of the stalk, holding the axe. As soon as Jack’s feet touched the ground, he took the axe and chopped and chopped. High above, the giant had just set one foot on the top leaf when the beanstalk creaked, swayed slowly, and fell across the fields like a long green bridge. The giant stayed sitting on his cloud, grumbling. Then he gave an enormous yawn, rolled over and took another nap.

That afternoon, the hen laid a golden egg, and Jack and his mother shared the gold with all their neighbors. The next morning, when they opened the door, they found Daisy grazing in the garden, plump and happy, with a colorful feather caught in her cowbell. No one ever saw the old man again.

From then on, the harp sang at every celebration in the village once more. And on stormy nights, when a long roll of thunder rumbles across the sky, Jack smiles and says to his mother, “That’s the giant, still snoring.”
