In a little village, between a river and a wood of birch trees, there lived a grandpa who had the loveliest vegetable garden for miles around. With him lived Grandma, their granddaughter Nina, a floppy-eared dog called Ginger and a black cat called Sooty. And under the woodpile, in a tiny house made of walnut shells, lived a mouse so small that everyone called him Pip.

One spring morning, when the almond trees were already in blossom, Grandpa went out to the garden with his hoe and his bag of seeds. He turned the soil until it was as soft as sponge cake and sowed turnip seeds in a neat row. But one little seed slipped through his fingers and rolled all the way to the sunniest corner of the garden, beside the fence.

Grandpa bent down, covered it carefully and whispered, “Grow, little turnip, grow nice and slow; may the sun keep you warm and the rain help you grow.”

Summer came, with its long evenings and its quick, noisy storms. The turnips in the row grew the way turnips do: not too much and not too little. But the one in the sunny corner grew and grew and never stopped. Its leaves became as big as umbrellas, and the hens hid underneath them when it rained. Poking out of the ground was a round piece of turnip, white and purple, that glowed at night like the moon.

When autumn came and the birch leaves turned yellow, Grandpa picked up a basket and said, “Today we harvest the turnips.” The ones in the row came out straight away with one little tug. But when he reached the sunny corner, Grandpa’s mouth fell open. That turnip was bigger than a barrel.

Grandpa rubbed his hands together, grabbed the leaves tight and shouted, “One, two, three, pull with me!” He pulled and pulled until he was as red as a tomato. But the turnip, as calm as could be, didn’t budge an inch.

“Grandma!” he called. “Come and help me!” Grandma, who was in the kitchen making blackberry jam, came out wiping her hands on her apron. She held on around Grandpa’s waist, and together they shouted, “One, two, three, pull with me!” They pulled with all their might, puffing like two teakettles. But the turnip, as calm as could be, didn’t budge an inch.

“Nina!” called Grandma. “Come and help us!” Nina, who was in the meadow with her kite, came running with her braids flying and grabbed hold of Grandma’s apron. “One, two, three, pull with me!” The three of them pulled. But the turnip, as calm as could be, didn’t budge an inch. In fact, Nina thought she heard it snoring.

“Ginger!” called Nina. “Here, good boy!” Ginger was busy burying a bone under the apple tree, but he came bounding over, ears flapping. He gently took Nina’s skirt in his teeth. “One, two, three, pull with me!” The four of them pulled. The ground creaked a little, but only a very little. The turnip, as calm as could be, didn’t budge an inch.

Ginger barked up at the garden wall, where Sooty was napping in the sun. The cat opened one eye, stretched very slowly and jumped down. “Meow,” he said, which in cat language means, “All right, but only for a minute.” He took hold of Ginger’s tail very carefully. “One, two, three, pull with me!” The five of them pulled with all their strength. But the turnip, as calm as could be, didn’t budge an inch.

They all sat down on the ground, out of breath. “This turnip is as stubborn as a mule,” sighed Grandma, and Grandpa scratched his beard, deep in thought.

Just then, a tiny voice piped up from the woodpile: “What if I help?” It was Pip, who had watched the whole thing with his bright little eyes. Ginger gave a snort of laughter. “You? But you’re smaller than a walnut!” Pip’s ears drooped, and he felt a bit embarrassed. But Nina crouched down and held out her hand. “Every bit of help counts here,” she said. “Come on, Pip, take your place at the end of the line.”

So they all lined up again. Pip held on to Sooty, Sooty held on to Ginger, Ginger held on to Nina, Nina held on to Grandma, Grandma held on to Grandpa, and Grandpa held on to the turnip leaves. They took a deep breath and shouted louder than ever: “One, two, three, pull with me!”

The ground shook. There was a long creak and then, pop! Out flew the turnip. Everyone tumbled backward, one on top of the other, in a heap of arms, paws, tails and giggles. And right at the top of the heap, sitting on the turnip like a king on his throne, was Pip.

It was the biggest turnip anyone had ever seen. It wouldn’t fit in the basket, or in the wheelbarrow, or in the kitchen. “So what do we do with it now?” asked Nina. Grandma smiled. “Now we make soup. And not just for us: for the whole village!”

The neighbors came to help. The blacksmith brought the giant cooking pot they used for festivals, the baker brought warm loaves, and the children brought carrots and potatoes from their gardens. In the village square, the soup bubbled all afternoon, and it smelled so good that the scent drifted all the way to the woods.

When evening came, they hung up lanterns and set out long tables. When the soup was ready, Grandpa picked up the ladle and said, “The first bowl goes to the one who made all the difference.” And he served Pip a thimble full of soup. The whole village clapped, and Pip was so happy that his whiskers stood on end.

The next spring, Grandpa planted his garden again. And in the sunniest corner, beside the fence, he left a tiny little row just for Pip. Ever since then, whenever something in that house is too heavy, everyone lines up, shouts “One, two, three, pull with me!” and always, always saves a place at the end for the smallest one.
