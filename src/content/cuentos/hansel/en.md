At the edge of a deep, wide forest lived a woodcutter and his two children, Hansel and Gretel. Their cottage was small, with a crooked chimney and a wooden bench beside the door. It had been a hard year, and all that was left in the pantry was a few potatoes and half a sack of flour.

“If we gather plenty of firewood and sell it at the market, we can buy bread for the whole winter,” their father said one morning. “Will you help me?” The children said yes at once. Gretel tied her boots with a double bow, and Hansel, who always liked to be prepared, filled his pockets with little white pebbles that gleamed like tiny moons.

“What are those for?” asked Gretel. “Just in case,” said her brother. And as they walked behind their father, Hansel quietly dropped a pebble here and another there.

Deep in the forest, their father began chopping dry branches, and the children stacked them in piles. Suddenly a butterfly fluttered past, with blue wings so shiny they looked like glass. “Look, Hansel!” They followed it from flower to flower, laughing, until it vanished among the leaves. Only then did they notice that they could no longer hear their father’s axe.

“Papa!” they called. Only the echo answered. The sun was starting to set, and the trees seemed taller than before. Gretel’s chin began to wobble, but Hansel took her hand. “Wait for the moon,” he said.

And when the moon rose, the little white pebbles lit up on the ground like a string of pearls. Pebble by pebble, step by step, the two children found their way home. Their father was standing at the door with a lantern in his hand. He had searched the whole forest and was about to set out again. He hugged them so tightly he almost lifted them off the ground.

A few weeks later, they needed more firewood. This time they left so early that Hansel had no time to look for pebbles. All he had in his pocket was a piece of bread for lunch. “I know,” he thought. And he dropped a trail of crumbs along the path.

While their father worked, Gretel found some brambles full of blackberries. “Let’s take a handful home for supper,” she said. But behind those brambles there were more, and behind those, more still, and when they tried to go back, the crumbs were gone. The forest birds had eaten every last one and were now singing happily in the branches.

That night they slept curled up together under a fir tree. At dawn, a little white bird landed on a branch and sang so sweetly that they followed it without a second thought. And that was how they came to a clearing with a cottage that smelled of cinnamon.

Its walls were made of soft bread, its roof of gingerbread, and its windows of clear sugar. The chimney was a candy stick. The children were so hungry that Hansel broke off a piece of the roof and Gretel nibbled a corner of the window. Crunch, crunch!

“Hey! Who’s eating my house?” said a voice. The door opened, and out came a short old woman with a grey bun, very thick glasses and a walking stick. At first she was kind. She gave them warm milk, pancakes with honey and two beds with white sheets. But the next morning she woke up in a very bad mood.

“You’ve ruined my roof,” she grumbled. “So now you’ll work for me. Forever!” She set Gretel to sweeping, carrying water and sticking new cookies onto the walls. And she locked Hansel in the pantry, which had a door made of bars and an enormous key. “You’ll eat in there until you’re good and strong,” she told him. “Then you can work twice as hard.”

The old woman had one weakness: sweets. She kept jars of candy all over the house and counted them every night. Gretel watched her for days, until she had an idea.

“Ma’am,” she said in her most innocent voice, “at the very back of the pantry there’s a huge jar of strawberry candies that isn’t on your list. Do you think someone might be eating them?” The old woman jumped. “My candies? What jar?” She opened the barred door and hurried inside, pushing sacks and boxes out of the way.

At that very moment, Hansel leaped out, and Gretel shut the door and turned the key. Click! “Let me out this instant!” the old woman protested. “When someone comes by, we’ll ask them to let you out,” said Gretel. “In the meantime, you have plenty of candy.” And she hung the key on a nail beside the front door.

Before they left, they filled a basket with bread, cookies and sugar. “We’ve earned it,” said Gretel. “We’ve worked all week.” And off they ran through the forest.

Before long, they came to a wide, calm river with no bridge and no boat. A white duck was swimming near the bank. “Duck, little duck,” Gretel asked, “would you carry us to the other side?” The duck nodded. “Hansel and the basket first, and then me,” she said, “because the two of us together are too heavy for you.” And the duck carried them across one at a time, slowly, over the sparkling water.

On the other side, the forest began to look familiar: the log where they used to stack the firewood, the path they always took. They ran until they saw the crooked chimney. Their father was sitting on the bench, holding the lantern in his hands. He had been searching for them for days without rest. When he saw them, he jumped to his feet and hugged them, crying with joy.

That night they ate soft bread by the fire, and the children told him everything: the butterfly, the crumbs, the gingerbread house and the white duck. And from then on, whenever they went into the forest, all three of them went together, and Hansel always carried little white pebbles in his pocket. Just in case.
