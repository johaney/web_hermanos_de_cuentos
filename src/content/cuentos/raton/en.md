In a field of sunflowers, inside the hollow trunk of a chestnut tree, there lived a mouse named Pipo. His house was small but very cozy: it had a bed of moss, a table made from a dried mushroom and a little round window where the morning sun came in. In the afternoons, Pipo liked to sit in his doorway and watch the clouds change shape.

Pipo had a cousin named Leopold who lived in the big city. One day Pipo wrote him a letter on a corn husk: “Dear cousin, would you like to spend a few days in the country? I’ll be waiting with open arms.” And a week later, Leopold arrived on the milkman’s cart, wearing a velvet waistcoat and a silk scarf and carrying a tiny suitcase.

Pipo welcomed him with a hug and took him for a walk. He showed him the stream, where the water sang between the stones; the meadow, which smelled of chamomile; and the hilltop, where you could see the whole valley. Leopold looked around and said, “Very nice, very nice,” although his shoes were getting muddy.

That evening, Pipo made supper with great care. He set out the best he had: some sunflower seeds, a handful of oats, a few blackberries from the bramble and a little piece of cheese he had been saving for a special occasion. “Not much at home, but peace of my own,” he said with a smile.

Leopold nibbled a seed and twitched his whiskers. “Cousin, this is very tasty, but don’t you get tired of eating the same things? In the city we have cakes, ham, grapes and sponge biscuits. You must come and see!” Pipo had never tasted cake, and he grew curious. “All right,” he said. “I’ll come with you.”

The next day, the two cousins climbed onto the milkman’s cart and rode all the way to the city. Pipo stared at everything with wide eyes. There were broad streets, towering lampposts, horses going clip-clop over the cobblestones and people rushing about in a terrible hurry.

Leopold lived in an enormous house, in a hole behind the dining-room cupboard. By the time they arrived it was night, and the family who lived there had just finished a grand dinner. On the long table were silver dishes with leftover roast chicken, trays of cakes, bunches of grapes and a huge cheese full of holes.

“Go ahead, cousin, help yourself!” said Leopold, climbing up the tablecloth. Pipo followed him and didn’t know where to begin. He tasted a crumb of cake, and it seemed to have sugar clouds inside. He picked up a grape so big that he had to hug it to take a bite.

But just as he was about to take his first real mouthful, the dining-room door creaked open. “Quick, into the hole!” cried Leopold. The two of them raced along the tablecloth, jumped to the floor and slipped behind the cupboard just in time. A maid came in with a candle, picked up some napkins and went out again.

When all was calm, the cousins went back to the table. Pipo, his heart still beating fast, took a little piece of cheese. But then they heard a “meow” very close by. A big grey cat had come into the dining room and was watching them from a chair, swishing her tail. “Into the hole!” cried Leopold again. And the two of them ran so fast that the cat only saw two little tails disappearing.

They waited a long while. Then, very slowly, they peeped out. The cat had gone off to sleep by the fireplace. But as soon as they set a paw on the tablecloth, they heard tremendous barking: “Woof! Woof!” It was the two dogs of the house, coming back from their evening walk and bounding down the hallway.

Pipo and Leopold hid behind the cupboard for the third time. They were safe, because neither the cat nor the dogs could fit through the hole, but Pipo was trembling from his whiskers to his tail. “Cousin,” he said at last, “thank you so much for inviting me. Your house is beautiful and your food is delicious. But I’d rather have my seeds in peace than a feast full of frights. Not much at home, but peace of my own.”

The next morning, Pipo said goodbye to Leopold with a hug and rode back to the country on the milkman’s cart. When he saw his chestnut tree in the distance, with the sunflowers swaying in the wind, his heart filled with joy. That night he had oats and blackberries for supper, and they tasted better than ever. Then he snuggled into his bed of moss and slept all night long.

But the two cousins never stopped loving each other. Ever since, in the spring, Leopold goes to the country to breathe the fresh air, splash in the stream and count clouds with Pipo. And in the winter, Pipo goes to the city to see the lamplights and try a cake or two, always keeping one eye out for the cat.

Each of them is happy in his own home, and they both know it. And whenever they say goodbye, Pipo always tells his cousin, “Come back soon. There isn’t much at my house, but there’s always room for you.”
