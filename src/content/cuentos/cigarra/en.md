At the edge of a wheat field, beneath an old olive tree, there lived a cicada named Clara. Her wings were as clear as glass, and her voice never grew tired. As soon as the sun warmed the earth, Clara would climb onto her favorite branch and begin to sing: “Sing, sing, the sun is king, the whole wide field is glittering!”

Close by, among the roots of the olive tree, was the entrance to an anthill. There lived an ant named Tula with hundreds of sisters. From sunrise to sunset, the ants marched to and fro in a line, carrying seeds, breadcrumbs and grains of wheat bigger than themselves.

One summer morning, Clara saw Tula go by with an enormous grain on her back. “Tula, put that down and come and sing with me! It’s a beautiful day.” Tula wiped her brow. “I can’t, Clara. Grain by grain, I work today and rest tomorrow. I have to fill the pantry before the cold comes.” Clara laughed. “The cold? But it’s July!”

The long golden days went by. The poppies opened and closed, the farmers cut the wheat, and the air smelled of warm straw. Clara sang in the morning, she sang at noon and she sang in the evening. The ants kept marching in their line without stopping, and sometimes, without even noticing, they wiggled their antennae in time to her music.

Autumn arrived. The vine leaves turned red and yellow, and the wind began to blow cooler. One afternoon, Tula passed the olive tree carrying an acorn. “Clara, shouldn’t you find a shelter and store some food?” The cicada yawned. “Tomorrow, Tula, tomorrow. There’s plenty of sunshine left.” But each day the sun came up a little later and went down a little sooner.

The first rains came all of a sudden. Clara had to shelter under a leaf, and her song grew quieter. She searched the field for seeds, but the ants and the birds had taken nearly all of them. All she found were a couple of shriveled blackberries. “Oh well,” she thought, “I’ll find more later.”

And then winter came. One morning, the field woke up white with frost. The olive tree stood still and silent, and the north wind whistled through its branches. Clara’s wings were frozen and her tummy was empty. She had no strength left to sing.

Shivering, she crept down the trunk to the entrance of the anthill. She hesitated for a moment, because she felt embarrassed. Then she called softly, “Tula? Are you there?” After a little while, two antennae peeped out. It was Tula, wearing a scarf knitted from spider silk.

“Tula,” said Clara in a very small voice, “you were right. I spent the whole summer singing and didn’t save anything. I’m so cold and so hungry. Could you help me?” Tula looked at her for a moment. She remembered the long summer days when Clara’s music had made the path feel lighter. “Come on in,” she said. “It’s nice and warm in here.”

The other ants weren’t so sure. “We’ve worked all year,” one of them muttered. “Why should we share with someone who hasn’t?” Tula answered, “Because we have enough for all of us. And because if it happened to us, we’d want someone to open the door.”

So Clara stayed in the anthill. They gave her a corner next to the pantry, a bowl of seeds and a blanket made of thistledown. Little by little, her wings grew warm and her voice came back.

Winter in the anthill was long and dark. The ants rested, but they got bored, and sometimes they felt sad, thinking about the sun. One night Clara asked them, “Would you like me to sing you something?” And she sang. She sang songs about poppies and wheat, golden mornings and summer evenings. The ants came closer, then began to tap their feet, and in the end they were all dancing in a ring through the tunnels.

From that day on, there was music in the anthill every night. The little ants learned to sing, and the old ones laughed as they hadn’t laughed in years. “We never knew music could warm you as much as a full pantry,” Tula told Clara.

Meanwhile, Clara was learning too. Tula showed her how to pick out the good seeds, how to keep them somewhere dry and how to count the days left before the cold. “Grain by grain,” Clara would repeat, “I work today and rest tomorrow.”

When spring came back at last and the sun warmed the olive tree, Clara came out of the anthill and stretched in the sunshine. That summer she sang as much as ever, but she also stored seeds in a hollow in the trunk. And the ants, as they worked in their line, sang along with her: “Sing, sing, the sun is king, the whole wide field is glittering!”

When the next winter came, Clara knocked on the anthill door once more. But this time she didn’t come empty-handed: she brought a little bag of seeds and a whole bundle of new songs.
