At the end of a very long winter, just as the snow was starting to melt from the rooftops, a traveler named Anselmo arrived in a small village. He wore a wide-brimmed hat, a green striped scarf and a backpack so full of patches that it looked like a quilt. An iron cooking pot hung from the backpack and went “clonk, clonk” with every step.

The village was very quiet. The shutters were closed, and no children were playing in the streets. It had been a hard winter: the cold had been so bitter that the harvest was small, and every family was keeping what little it had tucked away, like treasure.

Anselmo knocked on the first door. “Good afternoon. Could you spare something to eat for a tired traveler?” A woman peeked out and shook her head. “We have nothing to spare,” she said, and shut the door. At the second house, he heard the same. At the third, nobody even opened.

The traveler did not get cross. He stood for a while in the middle of the square, looking at all the closed houses, and thought, “This village isn’t hungry for bread. It’s hungry for being together.” Then he smiled, took off his backpack and began to get something ready.

First he gathered some dry sticks and lit a fire beside the fountain. Next he filled the pot with water and set it over the flames. Last of all, he took from his pocket a grey stone, round and smooth as an egg, polished it carefully on his sleeve and dropped it in. Plop!

A girl named Lucia, who had been watching him from behind a corner, couldn’t hold in her curiosity. “What are you cooking?” she asked. “Stone soup,” said Anselmo, stirring the water with a wooden spoon. “It’s the tastiest soup in the world. Water clear and stone so round, a pinch of smiles for all the town!”

Lucia ran to tell her mother, her mother told the neighbor, and the neighbor told anyone who would listen. Little by little, the shutters creaked open, and people came out into the square with their arms folded to see this very strange thing.

Anselmo tasted the soup and smacked his lips. “Mmm, nearly ready. Though an onion would make it even better.” Lucia’s mother thought about it. An onion wasn’t much. She went home and came back with a big fat one, which Anselmo chopped into little pieces and tipped into the pot.

“Water clear and stone so round, a pinch of smiles for all the town!” sang the traveler. He tasted it again. “Delicious! Although a couple of carrots would give it a lovely color.” The baker, who had a few carrots in his pantry, brought them without a word. And the pot began to smell like a vegetable garden.

Then Grandpa Ramiro turned up with three potatoes. Mrs. Remedios brought a handful of beans. The twins from the blue house came with a bunch of parsley and a bay leaf. Somebody brought salt, somebody brought a chunk of pumpkin, and at last somebody brought a ham bone that had been hanging in the kitchen all winter long.

With every new thing that went into the pot, Anselmo sang his song, and each time more voices joined in: “Water clear and stone so round, a pinch of smiles for all the town!” Meanwhile, the neighbors began to chat. They talked about how they had got through the winter, who had caught a cold and what they were planning to sow in the spring.

By the time the soup was ready, its smell had filled the whole village. Someone carried a long table into the square, someone else brought benches, and the children set out bowls and spoons. The baker appeared with two loaves of fresh bread, and Grandpa Ramiro brought out his violin, which he hadn’t played since the autumn.

They all ate together under the first stars. The soup was hot, thick and full of flavor, and there was plenty for second helpings. Lucia, her cheeks rosy from the fire, asked the traveler, “Does the stone really make it taste good?” Anselmo gave her a wink. “The stone is only for getting started. Everyone adds the flavor.”

That night, nobody closed their shutters. There was music, laughter and stories around the fire until very late, and Anselmo slept at Lucia’s house, on a mattress by the fireplace.

The next morning, the traveler put on his hat, hung the pot on his backpack and got ready to go on his way. Before he left, he took the smooth stone out of his pocket and placed it in Lucia’s hand. “It’s yours now,” he said. “If you ever see the doors in this village closing again, you’ll know just what to do.”

Lucia kept the stone in a little box on the shelf in her room. And they say that ever since, every year when winter ends, the neighbors meet in the square with an enormous pot. Everyone brings whatever they can, and they all sing together: “Water clear and stone so round, a pinch of smiles for all the town!”
