The Bible tells of a girl named Miriam who lived long, long ago in the land of Egypt. Her family was Hebrew, like many other families who lived beside the great River Nile. Miriam had a little brother, Aaron, and one day her mother shared some very special news: a new baby was on the way.

But those were not easy times. Pharaoh, the king of Egypt, was afraid that the Hebrew people were becoming too many. So he had given a very unfair order: newborn Hebrew boys were not allowed to stay with their families. Mothers and fathers were worried, and in many homes people spoke in hushed voices.

When the baby was born, he was a beautiful boy, with tiny fists and bright, wide-awake eyes. His mother held him close and decided she would do everything she could to protect him. For three months they hid him at home. Miriam sang him lullabies very softly so that he wouldn’t cry, rocked his cradle, and tickled his toes. “Hush, little brother,” she whispered. “I’ll look after you.”

But the baby kept growing, and his cries got louder and louder. One day his mother looked at Miriam with eyes full of love and said, “We can’t keep him hidden here anymore. I have an idea. But I’ll need your help.”

They went together to the riverbank, where tall papyrus reeds grew. They cut the longest, bendiest stems, and Mother began to weave a basket, passing one reed over and the next one under, just like braiding hair. Miriam handed her the stems one at a time. When the basket was finished, Mother coated it inside and out with pitch, a dark, sticky paste that smelled of pine resin. “Now not a single drop of water will get in,” she explained.

That morning, Mother wrapped the baby in a soft little blanket and laid him in the basket. She kissed his forehead and closed the lid carefully, leaving a gap for air. Then they went down to the river and set the basket floating among the reeds, in a quiet corner where the water hardly moved.

Mother had to go back home, her heart heavy. But Miriam stayed. She hid behind some tall bushes a little way off and sat down to keep watch. Miriam never took her eyes off the basket.

The river was full of life. Dragonflies hummed over the water, a heron stepped slowly along on its long legs, and frogs croaked among the leaves. The sun was warm, and the basket rocked gently, like a cradle. Every now and then, Miriam heard a little gurgle and smiled. Miriam never took her eyes off the basket.

Suddenly there were voices and laughter. Down the path along the bank came Pharaoh’s daughter, the princess of Egypt, with her maids. She was coming to bathe in the river. She wore a white linen dress and bracelets that jingled as she walked. Miriam held her breath.

The princess came to the water’s edge and spotted something among the reeds. “What’s that?” she asked. “Bring it to me, please.” One of the maids waded into the river, picked up the basket, and carried it to her. The princess lifted the lid, and at that very moment the baby began to cry.

The princess gazed at him tenderly. “Poor little thing,” she said softly. “He must be one of the Hebrew babies.” She lifted him out of the basket and cradled him in her arms, and little by little the baby grew calm.

Then Miriam did something very brave. She came out of her hiding place, brushed off her dress, and walked up to the princess. Her knees were trembling a little, but she spoke in a clear voice: “My lady, shall I go and find a Hebrew woman who can nurse the baby and look after him for you?”

The princess looked at her, looked at the baby, and nodded. “Yes, go.”

Miriam ran like the wind, her bare feet kicking up dust on the path. She burst into the house, out of breath. “Mother, come quickly! The princess has found him, and she needs someone to take care of him!” Her mother dropped everything and went with her to the river.

The princess handed her the baby. “Take care of this child for me,” she said, “and I will pay you for your work.” Mother held him tight against her chest, and both of them, mother and daughter, shed a tear of joy. No one else on the riverbank knew that this woman was really the baby’s mother. Only the two of them knew.

And so the little boy went back home and grew up with his family, listening to Miriam’s lullabies and Aaron’s laughter. When he was a bit older, his mother took him to the palace, and the princess welcomed him as her own son. She named him Moses, because, she said, she had drawn him out of the water.

Many years later, that baby from the basket would lead his people to freedom. And beside him, singing and playing her tambourine, would be Miriam, the big sister who once never took her eyes off the basket.
