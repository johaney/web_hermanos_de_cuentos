In a big house with a garden full of pumpkins lived a girl with her father, her stepmother and her two stepsisters, Rosaura and Clotilde. Her father was a merchant who went away on long journeys, so nearly all the housework fell to her.

On cold nights, the girl sat by the kitchen fireplace, so close that her skirt was always dusted with cinders. That was why her stepsisters started calling her Cinderella. She didn’t mind too much. “There are much worse names,” she would say, and laugh.

From sunrise on, the house was full of the same calls: “Cinderella, my ribbons!” “Cinderella, the tea is cold!” “Cinderella, I can’t find my fan!” And Cinderella hurried from room to room in her checked apron.

But work was not the only thing she was good at. She knew the song of every bird in the garden, she fixed the well’s pulley whenever it got stuck, and at night she read old books by the glow of the embers. Once, when a fox crept into the henhouse, she was the one who ran out in her nightgown with a broom and chased it off, while everyone else slept.

One morning, as she was drawing water from the well, an old woman in a patched cloak came up to her. “Could you spare a little water, my dear?” Cinderella filled a bowl for her, and seeing how thin she was, gave her the bread from her own lunch as well. The old woman smiled as if she already knew her, and went on her way down the road.

That very day, a messenger arrived from the palace. The king was holding a ball in honor of the prince, and every young woman in the kingdom was invited. What a fuss there was in the house! Rosaura wanted a dress with feathers, and Clotilde wanted one with a hundred bows. Cinderella sewed them both, curled their hair and buckled their shoes.

“May I go too?” she asked at last. Her stepmother looked her up and down. “In that apron covered in cinders? Besides, someone has to look after the house.” And the three of them climbed into the coach and drove away.

Cinderella went out into the garden and sat down among the pumpkins. The stars were starting to shine, but she didn’t feel like looking at them. Then she heard footsteps. It was the old woman from the well, but now her cloak glimmered as if it were stitched with moonlight.

“I am your fairy godmother,” she said. “And fairies never forget a bowl of water or a piece of bread. Would you like to go to the ball?” Cinderella’s eyes grew wide. “Of course I would!” “Then bring me the biggest pumpkin in the garden.”

The fairy touched it with a hazel twig, and the pumpkin began to grow and swell and shine until it became a golden carriage. Six green lizards sleeping on a stone turned into six cream-colored horses. A plump, good-natured toad became a coachman with a fine mustache. And Cinderella’s apron turned into a gown the color of the sky at dawn, pink and gold.

Last of all, the fairy gave her a pair of glass slippers that tinkled like little bells. “Just one thing,” she warned. “When the clock strikes twelve, everything will go back to how it was. Don’t forget.”

When Cinderella stepped into the ballroom, the music stopped for a moment. No one knew who she was, not even her stepsisters. The prince, a little tired of all the bowing and curtsying, came over to greet her. Cinderella didn’t talk to him about gowns or jewels. She talked about birds and books and stars, and told him the story of the fox and the broom. The prince laughed as he hadn’t laughed in a long time.

They danced all night. During a pause, a young page tripped and dropped a tray of pastries, and Cinderella was the only one who knelt down to help him. Afterward, she took two little cakes to Rosaura and Clotilde, who didn’t recognize her and thanked her in great surprise.

All of a sudden, the tower clock began to strike. Bong! Bong! Bong! Cinderella ran off without a word. On the palace steps, one of her glass slippers slipped off, but she didn’t stop. At the last stroke, the carriage became a pumpkin again, the horses turned back into lizards, and the coachman gave a hop and a croak. Cinderella walked home in her checked apron, with the other slipper tucked safely in her pocket.

The next day, the prince traveled across the kingdom with the lost slipper. “I want to find the girl who talked to me about the stars,” he said. But it didn’t fit anyone.

When he came to Cinderella’s house, Rosaura pushed and puffed, but the slipper was too short. Clotilde squeezed and sighed, but the slipper was too long. “There are no other girls here,” said the stepmother. Then Cinderella came out of the kitchen, brushed the cinders from her skirt, and said in a clear voice, “Yes, there is one. May I try it on?”

The stepsisters burst out laughing, but the prince said, “Everyone may try it.” And the glass slipper slid onto her foot as if it had been made just for her. Then Cinderella took the other slipper out of her pocket, and the prince recognized her smile. “It’s you!”

The stepmother, Rosaura and Clotilde fell very quiet and lowered their heads. “Please forgive us,” Clotilde said at last. “We haven’t treated you well.” Cinderella looked at them for a moment, and then she smiled. “I forgive you. But from today on, you each buckle your own shoes.”

When the prince asked her to marry him, Cinderella said she would like to get to know him better first. So they spent many afternoons walking in the garden, counting birds and stars. And when at last they were married, there in the front row, wearing a patched cloak, sat an old woman who smiled as if she had known all along.
